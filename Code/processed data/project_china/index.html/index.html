The Growing Demand

With a rise in population, as we see in the timelapse below, the whole world faced the same problem: feeding its inhabitants.
Since 1961, the global population has more than doubled, but this growth isn't distributed evenly. We need to establish where the mouths are located to understand the pressure on local versus global food systems.
We can spot that China, having had the biggest growth amidst all, had to quickly think of a solution to face this challenge.

In 1961, China was still a country marked by hunger: the population was growing fast, but the fields were not enough. In sixty years, however, the way the country provides food for its people has changed radically.
This project aims to offer viewers an insight into China’s extraordinary agricultural evolution and its powerful strategies for confronting one of humanity’s greatest challenge: global hunger.

The Efficiency Miracle

We focus here on cereal production: cereals are a core source of energy in most diets, accounting for over half of total caloric intake in many countries, and they also occupy the majority of global arable land by area.
With that being said, in the graph below the indices 'Production', 'Yield' and 'Area Harvested' refer to cereals.

Our increased output of cereal is not due to land expansion, which has played a very small role. Instead, the success is rooted in efficiency: yields have nearly tripled since 1961. This crucial improvement means that despite overall production growth, we now use less land per person than was required fifty years ago.

The Green Revolution-a crucial period for global agriculture beginning in the mid-20th century-marked the widespread transition from traditional to industrial practices in the developing world. This transformation was powered by the broad adoption of a package of new technologies, including:

  • High-yielding rice, wheat, and maize seed varieties.
  • Chemical inputs, such as fertilizers and pesticides.
  • New irrigation systems.
  • Farm mechanization.

This technological adoption brought a significant shift, though its implementation and success varied from country to country.
We will focus on China, considering its big growth.

The Mechanics of Growth

We used the Hans Rosling-style scatterplot to show the momentum of this change. This visualizes the relationship between how much we plant (Area) and how much we actually get (Production) over time, driven by technology (Yield).

We aim to illustrate changes in production volume and shifts in cropping typology.
The graph reveals a clear move toward vegetable cultivation. This shift is driven by advances in crop genetics, irrigation, greenhouse farming, and agricultural inputs (fertilizers, pest management), which have boosted vegetable productivity and made farming vegetables more economically viable, encouraging farmers to allocate more land to vegetables.
